Medicare Advantage by the Numbers: Enrollment, Denials, Lifetime Costs, and the Facts Behind the Debate

Austin TylerLicensed Medicare Advisor, Tyler Insurance Group · November 3, 2026 · 14 min read

Updated November 3, 2026. Every figure on this page comes from a public source (KFF, CMS, MedPAC, the HHS Inspector General, the Social Security Administration, the U.S. Senate) and is listed at the bottom. Where we do our own arithmetic, we say so and show the assumptions.

Few topics in Medicare start arguments faster than Medicare Advantage. Some people call it the best deal in retirement; others say they would never touch it. Much of what gets said in both directions is a story, a TV ad or a bad experience someone heard about. This page sets the stories aside and lays out the numbers: how many people use these plans, how often they deny care, why so many people distrust them, and what each way of getting Medicare actually costs over a lifetime. We are not here to pick a side. We are here so you can decide with facts.

The short version:

  • More than half of Medicare is now Advantage: 55% of eligible people, more than 1 in 2, over 35 million, in 2026.
  • Most prior authorization requests are approved. In 2024, plans denied 7.7%, about 1 in 13 requests. Only about 1 in 9 denials was appealed, and about 4 in 5 appeals won.
  • The distrust has real causes: documented denial problems in nursing-home and rehab care, hospitals leaving networks, aggressive marketing, and federal estimates that plans are paid more than Original Medicare would cost.
  • Supplements are getting harder to afford. The average Medigap premium rose 20% from 2020 to 2025, from $203 to $244 a month, about $490 more a year. Outside your first six months on Part B, most states let insurers turn you down for health reasons.
  • Over 20 years, a Plan G supplement plus a drug plan costs roughly $87k to $133k in premiums and deductibles, against median savings of $110,100 among people on Medicare. A $0-premium Advantage plan costs more only if your copays average more than about $4,300 to $6,700 a year, every year.

How many people are on Medicare Advantage?

In 2026, 55% of eligible Medicare beneficiaries are enrolled in a Medicare Advantage plan, according to KFF. Enrollment passed 35 million people in February. In 2007 the share was 19%. Growth has slowed to about 3% a year, and most of the recent growth is in Special Needs Plans for people with Medicaid or chronic conditions, which now hold 8.2 million people, nearly 1 in 4 enrollees. Two companies, UnitedHealth Group and Humana, cover 46% of all Advantage members.

So the first fact is simple: a majority of people who can choose are choosing it. That does not make it right for everyone, but it does mean the typical Advantage member is not a rare or confused exception.

Denials: what the data actually shows

Most Advantage plans require prior authorization for some services, meaning the plan must approve them before you get them. This is the heart of most complaints, so here is the full picture from KFF's analysis of CMS data.

Figure 1. Medicare Advantage prior authorization, all plans, 2024: 52.8 million requests, 7.7% denied, 11.5% of denials appealed, 80.7% of appeals overturned. Source: KFF analysis of CMS data. Per-1,000 figures are our arithmetic.

  • Volume: 52.8 million prior authorization requests in 2024.
  • Denials: 4.1 million fully or partly denied, 7.7%, about 1 in 13.
  • Appeals: only 11.5% of denials were appealed (about 1 in 9), and 80.7% of those appeals reversed the denial in full or in part (about 4 in 5).
  • Newer data: insurers' own published figures for 2025, covering 14 large companies, show 12% of standard requests denied in Advantage and 67% of appealed denials overturned. The method differs from the CMS data, so the two years are not directly comparable.

Two harder facts belong next to those. In 2022 the HHS Inspector General reviewed a sample of 2019 denials from 15 large plans and found that 13% of denied prior authorization requests met Medicare's own coverage rules, about 1 in 8, meaning Original Medicare likely would have paid. And in 2024 a U.S. Senate investigation found UnitedHealthcare's denial rate for post-acute care (skilled nursing, rehab) rose from 8.7% in 2019 to 22.7% in 2022, from about 1 in 11 requests to nearly 1 in 4, with post-acute denials at UnitedHealthcare and CVS running about three times their overall rates. The insurers dispute the report's conclusions.

Original Medicare is not denial-free either. It requires prior authorization for far fewer services, about 2 requests per 100 people in 2024, but it denied about 23% of those, nearly 1 in 4. Since January 2026, a federal pilot called WISeR has added prior authorization to traditional Medicare for selected services in six states (Arizona, New Jersey, Ohio, Oklahoma, Texas and Washington).

What the denial numbers mean for you: the large majority of requests are approved, but if you are denied, appeal. Most people do not, and most people who do, win.

Why so many people dislike Medicare Advantage

The anger is not just emotion. Each of the common complaints has a documented basis, and it is worth knowing which is which.

1Denials, especially for rehab and nursing care.The Inspector General and Senate findings above are the source of many stories people repeat, and the post-acute numbers are the strongest evidence behind them.
2Networks that change.Becker's Hospital Review tracked about 40 health systems dropping some or all Advantage contracts in 2025, and at least 25 more in 2026, often citing prior authorization and slow payment. If your hospital leaves your plan's network, your plan may no longer cover care there except in emergencies.
3The marketing.A 2022 Senate Finance Committee report found complaints to CMS about Advantage marketing more than doubled from 2020 to 2021. Many people's first impression of these plans is a late-night ad or a cold call, and that sticks.
4The cost to taxpayers.Medicare's own advisory commission, MedPAC, estimates that Advantage plans will be paid about 14% more in 2026 than Original Medicare would have spent on the same people, about $76 billion, roughly $2,200 per Advantage member a year. MedPAC also estimates it makes Part B premiums $11 billion higher in total, roughly $15 a month for each person on Part B (our arithmetic). The industry disputes the method. This is a real policy debate about the program's cost, separate from what your own plan charges you.
5It can be hard to go back.If you leave a supplement for an Advantage plan and later want the supplement again, insurers in most states can ask health questions and turn you down. Only Connecticut, Massachusetts, Maine and New York guarantee that right every year for people 65 and older. A federal trial right lets you return within 12 months of first joining an Advantage plan, but after that, the door may not open.

Now the other side of the ledger, also from the research. KFF's review of 62 studies comparing the two programs found few big differences across a wide range of measures. Satisfaction is similar: in one analysis of federal survey data, 94% of people in each program said they were satisfied with the quality of their care. And Advantage members are more likely than people in Original Medicare to have incomes below $20,000, which matters for the next section.

What seniors actually live on

Any honest comparison has to start with the budget, because for most people the budget decides.

  • The average retired worker's Social Security check is about $2,071 a month in 2026, rising to about $2,170 in January 2027. For 12% of men and 15% of women 65 and older on Social Security, about 1 in 8 men and 1 in 7 women, it is 90% or more of their income.
  • Half of all people on Medicare live on less than $43,200 a year per person and have less than $110,100 in savings. One in four has income below $24,600 and savings below $18,950.
  • Retirement accounts: only about half of households aged 65 to 74 have one at all. For those that do, the median balance is about $200,000.
  • Spending: households headed by someone 65 or older spent an average of $61,432 in 2024 on everything, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics. That is a household figure; the average retired worker's check is about $24,900 a year.
  • Health costs already take a big bite: in 2023 people on Medicare spent an average of $6,459 out of pocket on health care, including premiums. That equals 36% of the average Social Security income per beneficiary: about $1 of every $3, or more than four months of benefits.

Figure 2. 2026 figures: average retired-worker benefit $2,071 (SSA), Part B $202.90 (CMS), average Plan G premium $194 among current policyholders (KFF analysis of NAIC data), average stand-alone drug plan premium about $35 (CMS). Copays and other costs are not included.

That is the first fact that drives many people toward Advantage: before any copay, Original Medicare with an average supplement and drug plan takes about one dollar in five from the average check in premiums. A $0-premium Advantage plan takes about one in ten.

Supplement premiums: how much, and how fast they rise

Medicare Supplement (Medigap) plans are excellent coverage: Plan G pays nearly everything Original Medicare leaves behind, at any doctor in the country who takes Medicare. The issue is price, and price over time.

  • The average Medigap premium was $244 a month ($2,926 a year) among current policyholders in 2025, according to KFF's analysis of insurer data. Plan G averaged $194, ranging from $164 in New Mexico to $257 in New York.
  • Premiums rose 7% from 2024 to 2025 (about $16 a month) and 20% from 2020 to 2025 ($203 to $244 a month) on average; Plan G's average rose about 13% in 2025 alone, about $22 a month.
  • Industry analysis of 2026 rate filings found Plan G increases of roughly 12% to 26% at several of the largest insurers. On a $194 premium, that is about $23 to $50 more a month.
  • In most states, insurers price by attained age, so your premium also rises as you get older, on top of those yearly increases. KFF notes these policies are the cheapest at first and can be the most expensive over time.

And the timing problem: the only point at which every insurer must sell you a supplement at its standard rate, regardless of health, is your six-month Medigap open enrollment period, which starts when you are 65 and enrolled in Part B. Certain events, such as losing retiree coverage, also create a guaranteed right. Miss those windows, or leave later, and in most states a heart condition, diabetes or a cancer history can mean higher rates or a denial. For many people, "just get a supplement" is not an option they actually have.

So 20 years of a supplement, drug plan and deductible runs roughly $87k to $133k. For the median person on Medicare, that is somewhere between most of and more than their entire savings. For the one in four with under $18,950 saved, it is less than ten years of Plan G premiums alone at today's price.

None of that makes a supplement a bad buy. It buys near-total protection and freedom to see any doctor, and for someone who can comfortably afford it, that can be worth every dollar. It does explain why so many people on fixed incomes look elsewhere.

Original Medicare alone vs. a Medicare Advantage plan

Some people skip a supplement and stay on Original Medicare by itself: about 3.5 million people, 13% of everyone in traditional Medicare, have no supplemental coverage at all. On premiums, this looks identical to a $0 Advantage plan: you pay Part B and nothing else. The difference is what happens in a bad year.

  • Original Medicare alone: after the $283 Part B deductible you pay 20% of every outpatient bill ($20 of every $100), plus $1,736 for each hospital stay's benefit period, with no yearly limit.
  • Medicare Advantage: every plan must cap your in-network Part A and B costs. The 2026 average limit is $5,421; the legal maximum is $9,250 (rising to $9,850 in 2027).

Figure 4. 2026 rules, Part B services, in-network for the Advantage plan. Original Medicare: $283 deductible plus 20% of the rest, with no yearly cap. Advantage limits from KFF. Premiums are separate: Plan G's are what buy that $283.

It shows in people's lives. In KFF's analysis of federal survey data, people in Original Medicare with no supplement were the most likely of any group to report problems paying for care: 30%, about 3 in 10 (2018 data). People with a supplement were the least likely. Advantage members fell in between.

The case for a Medicare Advantage plan, in facts

Put the pieces together and the factual case for an Advantage plan with drug coverage (often called an MAPD) looks like this:

  • Low or no premium: about 3 in 4 people in these plans pay nothing beyond Part B. The average is $15 a month.
  • A hard yearly cap: unlike Original Medicare alone, your in-network costs stop at the plan's limit, $5,421 on average.
  • Drug coverage included, with the same $2,100 yearly drug cap (2026) as any Part D plan.
  • Benefits Original Medicare does not cover: more than 99% of members have some vision coverage, 98% dental and 95% hearing. How generous those benefits are varies widely by plan.
  • The break-even math: for a $0-premium plan to cost more than the supplement path over 20 years, your copays and coinsurance would have to average more than about $4,300 to $6,700 a year, every year, which is roughly the average plan's entire out-of-pocket limit. Public data do not show how many members reach their limit, but by definition, the years you do not reach it cost less.

What a Medicare Advantage plan does not do

Facts cut both ways, and you should weigh these before choosing:

  • Networks. Most plans work best, or only, with in-network doctors and hospitals, and networks can change each year.
  • Prior authorization. Some care needs approval first, and denials, while a minority, do happen.
  • A bad year costs more than a supplement. In a year you hit the cap, you pay up to the cap; with Plan G you would pay about $283.
  • Travel and second homes. Coverage away from home is usually limited to emergencies, unless the plan is a PPO or has a travel benefit.
  • The way back may close. Returning to a supplement later can depend on your health.

Common questions

How many people are on Medicare Advantage?

In 2026, 55% of eligible Medicare beneficiaries, more than 35 million people, are enrolled in Medicare Advantage, according to KFF. The share was 19% in 2007. About 8.2 million are in Special Needs Plans.

How often do Medicare Advantage plans deny prior authorization?

In 2024, Medicare Advantage plans fully or partly denied 7.7% of 52.8 million prior authorization requests, according to KFF's analysis of CMS data. Only 11.5% of denials were appealed, and 80.7% of appeals overturned the denial in full or in part. Insurer-published data for 2025 show 12% of standard requests denied.

Why do so many people dislike Medicare Advantage?

The main documented reasons are prior authorization denials (including findings by the HHS Inspector General and a 2024 Senate investigation into post-acute care), health systems leaving plan networks, aggressive marketing, federal estimates that plans are paid about 14% more than Original Medicare would cost, and the difficulty of returning to a Medigap supplement later because most states allow medical underwriting.

How much does a Medicare Supplement cost over a lifetime?

The average Medigap premium was $244 a month in 2025, and Plan G averaged $194. Using $160 a month at age 65 with yearly increases of 5% to 10%, plus a drug plan and the Part B deductible, 20 years costs roughly $87k to $133k, excluding the Part B premium. Actual costs depend on your state, insurer, age and rate increases.

Is Original Medicare alone cheaper than Medicare Advantage?

On premiums they can be the same, since both may cost only the Part B premium. The difference is risk: Original Medicare alone has no yearly limit on the 20% you pay, so a year with $100,000 of outpatient care would cost about $20,226, while a Medicare Advantage plan caps in-network costs at its limit, $5,421 on average in 2026.

Can I switch from Medicare Advantage back to a Medigap plan?

You can apply at any time, but outside your six-month Medigap open enrollment period and certain federal guaranteed-issue events, insurers in most states can ask health questions and deny you or charge more. Connecticut, Massachusetts, Maine and New York guarantee issue every year for people 65 and older. If you joined Medicare Advantage when first eligible, a federal trial right lets you switch back within 12 months.
