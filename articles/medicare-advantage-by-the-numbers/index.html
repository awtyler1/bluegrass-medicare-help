Medicare Advantage, by the Numbers

More than half of Medicare now runs through private plans, and few topics draw stronger opinions. We set the opinions aside and went through the public data on denials, costs and the alternatives.

Austin TylerLicensed Medicare Advisor, Tyler Insurance Group · November 3, 2026 · 13 min read

Disclosure: Tyler Insurance Group is an independent agency licensed to sell both Medicare Supplement and Medicare Advantage plans, and insurers pay us commissions on both. Every figure here comes from a public source listed at the end; where we calculate something ourselves, we say so.

Medicare Advantage now covers more than half of the people who are eligible for it, and it is the part of Medicare people argue about most. Supporters point to low premiums and a yearly spending cap. Critics point to denied care, narrow networks and aggressive sales. Both sides have evidence. The question is what the evidence says when you put it in one place.

The short answer: Advantage plans approve the large majority of requests, but their denials are real, uneven and rarely challenged. Supplements offer the strongest protection in an expensive year, but their premiums are rising faster than most retirees' incomes and, in most states, are not guaranteed to be available later. Which costs less over a lifetime depends mostly on how many expensive years you have, and the break-even point is further out than many people assume.

What we found

  1. 55% of eligible beneficiaries are in Medicare Advantage in 2026, up from 19% in 2007.
  2. Of every 1,000 prior authorization requests in 2024, plans denied 77. About 9 of those denials were appealed, and about 7 of the appeals succeeded.
  3. The average supplement premium rose 20% from 2020 to 2025, to $244 a month, and most states let insurers reject applicants for health reasons after the first six months on Part B.
  4. Over 20 years, a Plan G supplement with a drug plan costs an estimated $87k to $133k beyond the Part B premium. A $0-premium Advantage plan costs more only if the member reaches the average out-of-pocket limit in roughly 10 to 15 of those 20 years.
  5. In a single expensive year, a supplement costs least, an Advantage plan stops at its cap, and Original Medicare alone has no ceiling at all.

More than half of Medicare is now private

Figure 1. Source: KFF, Medicare Advantage in 2026: Enrollment Update and Key Trends. Enrollment passed 35 million in February 2026.

The growth has slowed, to about 3% in 2026, and most of it is in Special Needs Plans for people who also have Medicaid or chronic conditions; those plans now hold 8.2 million people, nearly a quarter of members. The market is concentrated: UnitedHealth Group and Humana together cover 46% of Advantage members. Whatever one thinks of the program, the typical member is not a rare exception. A majority of people who can choose are choosing it.

How often plans say no

Almost every Advantage plan requires prior authorization for some services, meaning the plan must approve them in advance. CMS now collects the counts, and KFF has analyzed them.

Figure 2. 52.8 million requests: 7.7% denied in full or part, 11.5% of denials appealed, 80.7% of appeals overturned in full or part. Source: KFF analysis of CMS data. Per-1,000 counts are our arithmetic.

Three things about these numbers matter. First, the denial rate counts requests, not people, and includes partial denials. Second, the low appeal rate is the striking figure: when people appeal, they usually win, which suggests many denials that stand are never tested. Third, the rate is rising by a newer measure. Insurers' own published figures for 2025, covering 14 large companies, show 12% of standard requests denied, or about 120 of every 1,000. That data set is built differently, so it is not a clean year-over-year comparison.

Two federal reviews explain much of the distrust. The HHS Inspector General examined a sample of 2019 denials from 15 large plans and found that 13% of the denied prior authorization requests met Medicare's coverage rules and likely would have been approved under traditional Medicare. And a 2024 U.S. Senate subcommittee report found that UnitedHealthcare's denial rate for post-acute care, such as skilled nursing and rehabilitation, rose from 8.7% of requests in 2019 to 22.7% in 2022, and that post-acute denials at UnitedHealthcare and CVS ran about three times their overall rates. UnitedHealthcare said the report mischaracterized its clinical practices; CVS said it does not use artificial intelligence to deny claims; the industry group AHIP said the report relied on cherry-picked anecdotes.

Traditional Medicare uses prior authorization far less, about 2 requests per 100 people in 2024, but denied about 23% of the requests it did review. Since January 2026, a federal pilot called WISeR has required prior authorization for selected services in traditional Medicare in Arizona, New Jersey, Ohio, Oklahoma, Texas and Washington.

Why the program draws so much criticism

Beyond denials, four documented issues drive most of the criticism:

  • Networks change. Becker's Hospital Review counted about 40 health systems dropping some or all Advantage contracts in 2025 and at least 25 in 2026, often citing prior authorization and slow payment.
  • The marketing. A 2022 Senate Finance Committee staff report found that beneficiary complaints to CMS about Advantage marketing more than doubled from 2020 to 2021.
  • The cost to the program. MedPAC, Congress's Medicare advisory commission, estimates Advantage plans will be paid about 14% more in 2026 than traditional Medicare would have spent on the same people, about $76 billion, adding an estimated $11 billion to total Part B premiums. The Better Medicare Alliance, an industry-backed group, called the estimate "one data point among many."
  • The door back can close. Outside a one-time six-month window after enrolling in Part B at 65, and certain events such as losing retiree coverage, insurers in most states can turn down supplement applicants for health reasons. Only Connecticut, Massachusetts, Maine and New York guarantee access every year for people 65 and older. A federal trial right allows a return within 12 months of first joining an Advantage plan.

The research on outcomes is more muted than the debate. A KFF review of 62 studies found few large differences between Advantage and traditional Medicare across a wide range of measures, and in one analysis of federal survey data, 94% of people in each program said they were satisfied with the quality of their care.

The budget most retirees are working with

Coverage decisions are budget decisions, so the household numbers come first. The figures below are medians, the midpoint, where available, because averages are pulled up by the wealthiest retirees.

  • Income and savings: half of all people on Medicare live on less than $43,200 a year per person and have less than $110,100 in savings. A quarter have income below $24,600 and savings below $18,950.
  • Social Security: the average retired worker receives about $2,071 a month in 2026, or about $24,900 a year, rising about 3.6% in January 2027. For 12% of men and 15% of women 65 and older on Social Security, it is at least 90% of their income.
  • Retirement accounts: about half of households aged 65 to 74 have one. Among those that do, the median balance is about $200,000.
  • Health costs: people on Medicare spent an average of $6,459 out of pocket on health care in 2023, including premiums, which KFF calculates as 36% of average Social Security income per beneficiary.

Figure 3. Part B $202.90 a month (CMS); Plan G $194, the average among current policyholders (KFF analysis of NAIC data); stand-alone drug plan about $35 (CMS). About 3 in 4 Advantage drug-plan members pay no premium beyond Part B (KFF). Copays are not included.

Advantage members are more likely than people in traditional Medicare to have incomes below $20,000 a year. For households at that level, a premium gap of roughly $2,750 a year is a large share of the budget, which helps explain where the enrollment growth has come from.

Supplements: strong coverage, rising price

A Medicare Supplement such as Plan G pays nearly everything traditional Medicare leaves behind, at any doctor in the country who accepts Medicare. The trade-off is the premium, and its direction.

Figure 4. Source: KFF analysis of NAIC data via Mark Farrah Associates. Up 7% from 2024 to 2025. Industry analysis of 2026 filings found Plan G increases of roughly 12% to 26% at several large insurers.

Plan G averaged $194 a month in 2025, from $164 in New Mexico to $257 in New York, and its average rose about 13% in that one year. Most states allow insurers to price policies by attained age, so the premium also climbs as the policyholder gets older, on top of the yearly rate increases. KFF notes these policies tend to be the cheapest at first and the most expensive over time. Combined with medical underwriting after the first six months, that is why "just buy a supplement" is not a real option for many people who did not buy one at 65.

Twenty years, three ways

To compare lifetime costs we modeled 20 years, from 65 to 85, using national averages. The Part B premium is left out because every path pays it. Because future premium increases are uncertain, the supplement path is shown as a range, and the Advantage plan is tested against a deliberately harsh assumption.

Figure 5. Our estimate, inputs in the methods box below. The dotted line is a worst case for the Advantage plan; most members do not reach their limit every year. The bottom line, $0, is an Advantage year with no care.

The chart brackets what an Advantage plan can cost. In a year with no care, it costs nothing beyond Part B, the bottom line. If a member reached the plan's average out-of-pocket limit, $5,421, every single year for 20 years, which almost no one does, costs would follow the dotted line. The supplement path lands between the two, and it is predictable: you pay it whether or not you use care. So the useful question is how many expensive years it takes for the Advantage plan to cost more.

A single bad year looks different, and this is where supplements earn their price:

Figure 6. Part B services, in-network for the Advantage plan. Original Medicare: $283 deductible plus 20% of the rest. Advantage limits for 2026 from KFF. Premiums are separate.

Traditional Medicare on its own, with no supplement, matches a $0 Advantage plan on premiums but has no ceiling on the 20% coinsurance. About 3.5 million people, 13% of those in traditional Medicare, have no supplemental coverage. In KFF's analysis of federal survey data, they were the group most likely to report problems paying for care, at 30% in 2018, while people with supplements were the least likely and Advantage members fell in between.

What the numbers say about who comes out ahead

Put together, the data point to fairly clear patterns:

  • An Advantage plan with drug coverage costs the least in most years, caps the bad ones, and includes extras that traditional Medicare does not cover: more than 99% of members have some vision coverage, 98% dental and 95% hearing. The trade-offs are networks, prior authorization, and a harder path back to a supplement.
  • A supplement with a drug plan costs the most in a typical year and the least in a bad one, with the freedom to see any doctor who takes Medicare. Its risk is the premium trend, which compounds over decades.
  • Traditional Medicare alone carries the most risk: the same premium as a $0 Advantage plan, with no cap. It works mainly for people with other coverage, such as retiree benefits, Medicaid or VA care.

How we did this

Data. Enrollment, prior authorization, premiums, out-of-pocket limits and supplemental benefits come from KFF research using CMS and NAIC data; payment estimates from MedPAC; denial reviews from the HHS Inspector General and the Senate Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations; income and savings from KFF and the Urban Institute; Social Security figures from the Social Security Administration. Each figure was confirmed in at least two sources or taken from the primary government source.

The 20-year model. Our own estimate, with these inputs:

  • Plan G at $160 a month at 65, the typical national quote for a new enrollee in 2025, rising 5%, 8% or 10% a year to reflect both age and rate increases (the 2025 average increase was 7%; 2026 filings at large insurers ran about 12% to 26%).
  • A stand-alone drug plan at $35 a month (the CMS 2026 average) and the $283 Part B deductible, each rising 5% a year.
  • Advantage out-of-pocket limits of $5,421 (2026 average) and $9,250 (2026 legal maximum), each rising 5% a year, with a $0 premium beyond Part B, as for about 3 in 4 Advantage drug-plan members.
  • Drug costs under the Part D cap are treated as the same on both paths. Dollar amounts are not adjusted for inflation.

What the data cannot tell us. How many Advantage members reach their out-of-pocket limit in a typical year; how care quality differs for a specific condition or plan; and what any one person will pay, which varies widely by state, insurer, age and health.

Common questions

How many people are on Medicare Advantage?

In 2026, 55% of eligible Medicare beneficiaries, more than 35 million people, are enrolled in Medicare Advantage, according to KFF. The share was 19% in 2007. About 8.2 million are in Special Needs Plans.

How often do Medicare Advantage plans deny prior authorization?

In 2024, plans denied 77 of every 1,000 prior authorization requests (7.7% of 52.8 million), fully or partly, according to KFF's analysis of CMS data. About 9 of those 77 denials were appealed, and about 7 of the appeals succeeded. Insurer-published data for 2025 show about 120 of every 1,000 standard requests denied.

Why do so many people dislike Medicare Advantage?

The main documented reasons are prior authorization denials, including Inspector General and Senate findings on care that should likely have been approved and on post-acute care; health systems leaving plan networks; aggressive marketing; MedPAC's estimate that plans are paid about 14% more than traditional Medicare would cost; and the difficulty of returning to a supplement later in most states.

How much does a Medicare Supplement cost over a lifetime?

The average Medigap premium was $244 a month in 2025, and Plan G averaged $194. In our 20-year estimate, Plan G starting at $160 a month at age 65 and rising 5% to 10% a year, plus a drug plan and the Part B deductible, costs about $87k to $133k beyond the Part B premium. Actual costs depend on state, insurer, age and rate increases.

Is Original Medicare alone cheaper than Medicare Advantage?

On premiums they can be the same, since both may cost only the Part B premium. The difference is risk: Original Medicare alone has no yearly limit on the 20% coinsurance, so a year with $100,000 of outpatient care would cost about $20,226, while a Medicare Advantage plan caps in-network costs at its limit, $5,421 on average in 2026.

Can I switch from Medicare Advantage back to a Medigap plan?

You can apply at any time, but outside your six-month Medigap open enrollment period and certain federal guaranteed-issue events, insurers in most states can ask health questions and deny you or charge more. Connecticut, Massachusetts, Maine and New York guarantee issue every year for people 65 and older. If you joined Medicare Advantage when first eligible, a federal trial right lets you switch back within 12 months.
