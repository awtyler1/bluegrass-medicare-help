# MA dental coinsurance: research record

Evidence base for `/articles/medicare-advantage-dental-coinsurance-kentucky/`. Compiled
2 October 2026. Corroboration standard: two to three independent sources per figure.

## 1. The shift to coinsurance, nationally

| Finding | Figure | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Share of individual MA plans offering dental, 2026 | 98% | KFF, Medicare Advantage 2026 Spotlight |
| Plans change dental parameters year to year (annual maximums, cost sharing) | stated explicitly | KFF, Medicare and Dental Coverage: A Closer Look |
| Typical cost sharing on major restorative work | up to 50% coinsurance | KFF; U.S. News |
| Average annual dental cap, 2026 | about $1,300 to $1,500 | KFF-derived secondary reporting; Enable Dental |
| Non-Medicare-covered supplemental benefit value, 2025 to 2026 | down about $7 per member per month | Milliman, State of the 2026 MA industry |
| Standalone comprehensive dental limits, 2025 to 2026 | down about 8% | Milliman |
| Shared (combined) dental limits, 2025 to 2026 | up about 6% | Milliman |
| Pattern described | preventive fully covered, major work with significant cost sharing | Milliman; ATI Advisory CY2026 supplemental benefit trends |
| OTC allowance average, 2026 | down about 13% to about $23 a month | Milliman |
| Plans offering non-emergency transportation | 31% (2025) to 24% (2026) | Milliman |

**Why carriers are doing it** (as reported, not our inference): higher medical cost trend and
tighter CMS payment growth in the 2025 and 2026 rate years, with carriers "prioritizing
benefits that align with member need." Coinsurance also shares utilization cost with the
member and makes the headline maximum last longer, which lets a plan advertise a larger
number. The article says both of those plainly.

**Kentucky plan-level dental design data is not available from the landscape file.** It
requires the CMS Plan Benefit Package (PBP) files. The article relies on national data plus
Austin's stated observation from client ANOCs, labeled as such.

## 3. The worked example (arithmetic, not a plan quote)

$1,000 annual allowance. $500 crown, in network, allowance otherwise unused.

| | No coinsurance | 25% coinsurance | 50% coinsurance |
|---|---|---|---|
| You pay at the chair | $0 | $125 | $250 |
| Plan pays | $500 | $375 | $250 |
| Allowance left | $500 | $625 | $750 |
| A second $500 crown: you pay | $0 | $125 | $250 |
| After two crowns, allowance left | $0 | $250 | $500 |
| A third $500 crown: you pay | $500 (allowance gone) | $250 ($250 plan, then you) | $250 |

Assumes the plan's paid share counts against the maximum, which is the common design. Some
plans apply the maximum differently; the article says to confirm in the Evidence of Coverage.
The 25% case is the example Austin asked for; 50% is shown because KFF reports it as common.

## 4. Mechanics the article states

- Preventive (cleanings, exams, x-rays) is usually $0 and may or may not count against the
  allowance; comprehensive (fillings, crowns, root canals, extractions, dentures, implants
  where covered) is where coinsurance appears.
- Frequency limits (a crown on the same tooth once in five years is typical), waiting periods
  on some plans, in-network requirement with a dental network that can be administered
  separately from the medical network.
- A **pre-treatment estimate** (predetermination) is a standard dental practice: the dentist
  sends the proposed treatment to the plan and gets back what it will pay.
- Where to look: Summary of Benefits "Comprehensive dental" row; Evidence of Coverage
  benefits chart, dental section; Annual Notice of Change benefits table each September.
